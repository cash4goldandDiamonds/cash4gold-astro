Compare the same items and transaction terms

A Los Angeles jewelry collection can include a heavy gold bracelet, a diamond ring, inherited pieces and a luxury watch. One total price gives you little information about how those different items were considered. Ask for a breakdown that lets you distinguish metal, stones and any value assigned to the complete piece. Record the date and any conditions attached to the offer.

Describe the same items to each prospective buyer. Include known repairs, missing components, altered settings and the records you have. An estimate made from a photograph or phone description can change after inspection. Treat it as an initial discussion rather than a commitment to pay an exact amount.

Before arranging a visit, confirm that the business buys the particular items you want to sell. A store that buys gold by weight may have a different process for an intact signed piece, while a watch discussion may depend on its reference, originality and service history. Ask how the complete item will be considered before allowing any disassembly.

Separate gold characteristics from gemstone information

Bring hallmarks, receipts and any existing records, but leave the jewelry intact. The FTC’s precious-metal jewelry guide explains distinctions among gold fineness and plated products. Those descriptions help you prepare questions; a visible stamp alone does not establish what an individual purchase offer should be.

For a mounted diamond, ask what can be assessed while the stone is in its setting and whether additional examination is proposed. A grading report records characteristics; it is not a promise of a resale price. GIA describes the 4Cs as color, clarity, cut and carat weight. Bring an existing report so the item and its identifying details can be discussed together.

Bring records without confusing their purpose

Keep available receipts, grading reports, prior appraisals, original boxes and watch papers with the corresponding pieces. Records can provide context, but different documents serve different purposes. An insurance replacement appraisal is not the same as an immediate purchase offer for a used item.

GIA’s jewelry-selling guidance discusses appraisals and alternative selling routes. If you need an appraisal for insurance, tax, estate or another formal purpose, tell the appraiser what the document must address. Do not assume that a buyer’s informal evaluation fulfills that requirement.

For inherited jewelry, avoid polishing, dismantling or discarding components before a discussion about the whole piece. Keep any maker’s packaging or documentation you already have. If there are several items, make a simple list and photographs for your own records so you can keep track of the pieces being considered.

Questions to ask about an offer

  • Does the offer cover the complete piece, or only particular materials?
  • Which weights, markings, stone characteristics and condition observations were considered?
  • Are any fees or deductions included, and what net amount is proposed?
  • How long is the offer available, and what could change it?
  • What payment method and timing would apply if you accept?
  • If you choose not to sell, how will your items and records be returned?

Keep the answers with the offer. A comparison is most useful when you can identify the same items, the same scope and the same conditions. You can take time to consider whether the proposed transaction suits you.

Questions about selling jewelry

Can I discuss inherited jewelry without a grading report?

Yes. Bring the complete pieces and any records you have. Explain what is known and unknown about the items; a report is useful context, but its absence does not prevent an initial discussion.

Is same-day payment guaranteed?

No timing should be assumed from an article or phone estimate. Confirm an accepted offer, the payment method and the expected timing for that transaction before proceeding.

Should an intact gold piece be treated only as scrap?

Ask how the complete piece is being considered before deciding. Its condition, design, components and identifying records may be relevant to the buyer’s assessment; a metal-only estimate and an offer for the intact item are different proposals.
